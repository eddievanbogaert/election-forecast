## Tier 1: Top Battlegrounds

These races are most likely to determine Senate control.

### North Carolina — Open Seat | R+3 PVI
**Rating: Likely D** · Polling avg: D+7 · D win prob: ~76%

Thom Tillis is not seeking re-election. Democrats recruited Roy Cooper, the two-term former governor with high statewide name recognition — their strongest possible candidate. The Republican nominee is Mark Whatley, who emerged from the March 3 primary. Cooper leads Whatley by 1–10 points across seven polls (averaging D+7), with the trendline moving in Cooper's direction over the past month. One outlier (TIPP, D+24) was excluded. This is the Democrats' top pickup opportunity.

**Key dynamics:** Cooper's personal brand consistently runs ahead of the generic D ballot in NC. Whatley has not yet consolidated the Republican base. If the national environment holds at D+3.5, Cooper's margin could widen further.

### Georgia — Ossoff (D) | R+4 PVI
**Rating: Lean D** · Polling avg: D+5.3 · D win prob: ~70%

Jon Ossoff won his 2021 runoff by just 1.2 points, making him the GOP's top target this cycle. However, early polling shows him leading all potential Republican opponents — Earl "Buddy" Carter, Doug Collins, and Brian Dooley — by 3–8 points. The Republican primary field remains unsettled, which limits R fundraising and message discipline.

**Key dynamics:** Ossoff benefits from incumbency and a stronger fundraising apparatus than in 2020. Georgia's competitive electorate cuts both ways: Ossoff over-performed PVI in 2021 but did so in an unusual special-election environment. A strong Republican nominee who consolidates suburban Atlanta voters could tighten this race considerably.

### Maine — Collins (R) | D+3 PVI
**Rating: Toss-up** · Polling avg: D+2.5 · D win prob: ~55%

Susan Collins has historically outperformed her state's PVI by 10+ points, and she remains one of the few Republicans who can win in a blue-leaning state. On the Democratic side, the primary is unsettled: Jason Platner leads Collins by 7 in one early poll, while Collins leads former Governor Janet Mills by 2 in another. The blended average stands at D+2.5.

**Key dynamics:** This race embodies a classic fundamentals-vs-polling tension. Collins's crossover appeal is real, but the national environment and her state's PVI both favor Democrats. The Democratic primary outcome matters enormously — a strong nominee could push this into Lean D territory; a weaker one could flip it to Lean R.

### Michigan — Open Seat | R+1 PVI
**Rating: Toss-up** · No polling · D win prob: ~55%

Gary Peters is retiring, creating a top-tier open-seat battleground in a true swing state. The Democratic primary features State Senator Mallory McMorrow, Rep. Haley Stevens, and Abdul El-Sayed. The Republican field is still forming. No general election polling exists yet; the model currently relies on fundamentals (R+1 PVI offset by the D+3.5 national environment).

**Key dynamics:** Michigan swung sharply between parties in recent cycles — this race will be a pure bellwether for the national mood. McMorrow has built a national profile through viral messaging on culture-war issues; Stevens has the congressional incumbency advantage; El-Sayed appeals to the progressive base. The primary outcome will significantly shape general election competitiveness.

### New Hampshire — Open Seat | D+1 PVI
**Rating: Lean D** · No polling · D win prob: ~62%

Jeanne Shaheen is retiring. Rep. Chris Pappas is the Democratic frontrunner. The Republican primary features two high-profile candidates: former Senator John Sununu and former Massachusetts Senator Scott Brown. No general election polling has been released; the model favors Democrats slightly on fundamentals.

**Key dynamics:** Both Sununu and Brown carry strong name recognition, but Brown's carpetbagger narrative (he represented a different state) is a liability. If Sununu wins the R primary, this race could tighten to a true toss-up. Pappas is a solid but unspectacular recruit in a state that rewards moderation.

## Tier 2: Competitive but Structurally Favored

These races are competitive in polling but face significant structural headwinds for the trailing party.

### Alaska — Sullivan (R) | R+9 PVI
**Rating: Lean R** · Polling avg: D+2 · D win prob: ~40%

Mary Peltola, the former at-large congresswoman who lost her House seat in 2024, is challenging Dan Sullivan. Recent polls show Peltola leading by 1–4 points despite an R+9 PVI — a significant overperformance. Alaska's ranked-choice voting system and history of electing independents and crossover candidates (Lisa Murkowski's 2010 write-in win, Peltola's 2022 House victory) make this plausible.

**Key dynamics:** The fundamentals heavily favor Sullivan — R+9 is a wide margin to overcome — but Peltola's personal brand and RCV create a real pathway. If the national environment strengthens beyond D+3.5, this becomes a genuine toss-up. Sullivan has not faced a serious challenge before and may be slow to ramp up.

### Ohio Special Election — Vance Vacancy | R+8 PVI
**Rating: Likely R** · D win prob: ~30% (est.)

J.D. Vance vacated this seat upon becoming Vice President. The special election pits former Senator Sherrod Brown (D) against Lt. Governor Jon Husted (R). Brown's return is significant — he held this seat from 2007 to 2025 and is one of the few Democrats who can win statewide in Ohio. However, he lost re-election in 2024, and the state has trended sharply rightward.

**Key dynamics:** Brown's populist economic brand remains strong in blue-collar Ohio, but replicating his past coalition will be difficult without a presidential-year electorate. Special election turnout dynamics are uncertain. Husted is a conventional Republican with statewide experience but less personal magnetism. R+8 PVI plus the 2024 loss make this an uphill climb for Brown.

### Florida — Moody (R, appointed) | R+5 PVI
**Rating: Lean R** · D win prob: ~35% (est.)

Marco Rubio vacated this seat upon becoming Secretary of State. Governor DeSantis appointed Ashley Moody, the former Attorney General. Democrat Val Jenkins is the likely nominee. Florida has trended rightward in recent cycles, but an appointed incumbent lacks the electoral mandate of someone who won the seat outright.

**Key dynamics:** Moody has statewide name ID from her AG tenure but has never run in a competitive general election. Jenkins is relatively unknown. Florida's R+5 PVI and Republican voter registration advantage make this structurally favorable for Moody, but appointed senators historically underperform elected ones. Democrats will need a strong nominee and favorable turnout to compete.

### Minnesota — Open Seat | D+3 PVI
**Rating: Lean D** · No polling · D win prob: ~65% (est.)

Tina Smith is retiring. The Democratic primary features Lt. Gov. Peggy Flanagan and Rep. Angie Craig, with early (pre-primary) polls showing Flanagan ahead. The August primary will clarify the field. Minnesota's D+3 PVI and the national environment both favor Democrats, but open seats are inherently more competitive.

**Key dynamics:** Flanagan would be the first Native American woman elected to the U.S. Senate, which could drive enthusiasm and fundraising. Craig has a moderate profile that may play better in a general election. Republicans have not won a Senate race in Minnesota since 2002.

## Tier 3: Watchlist

These races are rated Safe but show unusual polling or structural features worth monitoring.

### Texas — Cornyn (R) | R+10 PVI
**Rating: Safe R** · Polling avg: R+1 · D win prob: ~15%

The Republican primary is headed to a runoff between Ken Paxton and John Cornyn — a bruising intra-party fight that could depress R general election enthusiasm and fundraising. Democrat James Talarico polls within 1–2 points of both R candidates. However, there is an important sourcing caveat: PPP polls (D-sponsored) show Talarico +2/+1, while UH Hobby polls (neutral) show R+1/R+2. The blended average is R+1.

**Why it's still Safe R:** R+10 PVI is an enormous structural advantage. Texas has not elected a Democrat to statewide office since 1994. The close polling likely reflects the primary disarray more than a genuine D opportunity. Monitor for post-runoff movement.

### Nebraska — Ricketts (R, appointed) | R+22 PVI
**Rating: Safe R** · Polling avg: R+1 · D win prob: ~8%

Dan Osborn, an independent, is challenging Pete Ricketts, who was appointed in 2023 after Ben Sasse's resignation. Osborn nearly defeated Deb Fischer in 2024 and remains a strong populist candidate. Polls show Ricketts +1 despite an R+22 PVI — a staggering gap between fundamentals and polling.

**Why it's still Safe R:** R+22 is virtually insurmountable in a normal environment. Osborn would likely caucus with Democrats for control purposes (and is modeled on the D side accordingly), but the fundamentals should dominate at this distance from Election Day. If polls hold through summer, this could be upgraded to Lean R.

## National Environment Model

**Net shift: D+3.54** (as of 2026-03-10)

The national environment is estimated through a four-component model that replaces the previous flat D+3.0 midterm penalty assumption.

| Component | Value | Contribution | Source / Coefficient |
|-----------|-------|-------------|----------------------|
| Base midterm penalty | — | D+1.50 | Historical average for president's opposing party |
| Presidential approval | Trump net approval: −11.0 | D+1.32 | 0.12 per approval point below zero (RCP avg, 16 polls) |
| GDP growth | 2.3% | D−0.09 | 0.3 per point above 2.0% trend (near-neutral) |
| Consumer sentiment | 64.7 | D+0.81 | 0.04 per point below 85.0 baseline |

**Interpretation:** The environment is moderately favorable to Democrats, driven primarily by the standard midterm penalty and negative presidential approval. Economic fundamentals are roughly neutral — GDP growth is near-trend, but consumer sentiment remains well below historical norms, suggesting voters feel worse about the economy than top-line growth would predict.

## Model Methodology

The forecast uses a Monte Carlo simulation framework with 40,000 iterations per run.

**State-level estimates** are a weighted blend of polling data and structural fundamentals. Polling weight ramps linearly from 0% (365+ days out) to 100% (on Election Day). At ~601 days out, the current weighting is approximately **35% polling / 65% fundamentals**, meaning structural factors (PVI, incumbency, candidate quality) still dominate.

**Correlated national error** is modeled with σ = 2.5, representing a uniform partisan wave that shifts all states simultaneously in each simulation. This captures the possibility that the national environment could be meaningfully better or worse for either party than the point estimate suggests.

**Key limitation:** At 601 days out, these probabilities carry substantial uncertainty. Historical Senate races show large shifts between early forecasts and Election Day outcomes. Races currently rated Lean or Toss-up should be treated as genuinely uncertain, and even Likely-rated races carry meaningful upset risk.

## Path to Democratic Control

Democrats need 51 seats for an outright majority (or 50 with a Democratic VP, which they do not have). Starting from 47 seats, they need a **net gain of 4**.

**Most likely pickup combination (if Democrats win control):**

1. **NC** (Likely D, ~76%) — Strongest pickup opportunity
2. **GA** (Lean D, ~70%) — Ossoff holds in a competitive state
3. **ME** (Toss-up, ~55%) — Collins falls in a blue-leaning state
4. **MI** (Toss-up, ~55%) — Open seat in a swing state

This four-race sweep has a naive joint probability of roughly 16% (assuming independence), which aligns with the model's ~12% control probability (the difference reflects correlated outcomes and the possibility of losing a currently-held seat).

**Stretch targets** that could substitute if one of the above falls short: AK (~40%), OH-Special (~30%), FL (~35%).

**Risk of losing held seats:** All currently D-held seats outside the battleground tier are Safe D. The primary defensive concern is the national environment shifting against Democrats, which would pressure GA and any open seats.
